## Abstract

Formal Contract-Driven Development (FCDD) organises an AI coding agent's work
around a machine-checked contract. Its purpose — the reason the method exists —
is **predictability**: a repair pinned to a kernel-checked specification should
land in a narrow band of behaviour and cost, and should not drift into
hallucinated or unfounded changes the way an unconstrained agentic session can.
This study asks two questions in that order: does the pinning work, and what
does it cost? We ran a controlled comparison on a 7,343-line Z80 assembly chess
engine into which an independent agent seeded seven single-byte faults, each
with a sealed acceptance test written before any repair was attempted. Two arms
fixed every fault: ordinary development-plus-review, and FCDD working from a
pre-built machine-checked contract.

**On cost the answer is unambiguous.** FCDD was more expensive on **7 of 7**
matched pairs (exact two-sided sign test *p* = 0.016 — the study's only
statistically supportable result), with a median ratio of **4.74×** on raw
tokens (the pre-registered primary metric), 3.40× on platform dollars and
3.28× at pre-registered rates; the tightest nonparametric interval reaching
95% at *n* = 7 is the full observed range, [1.12, 6.88]. The upfront contract
($21.75) never amortised, because FCDD was cheaper on no fault — and it held a
free pristine-derived oracle its competitor lacked.

**On predictability the data is suggestive and insufficient.** Across the
seven defects, ordinary development's cost spread is 6.2× against FCDD's 2.3× —
but the entire contrast comes from one cell, and excluding it the two arms'
spreads are **identical at 2.31×**. Replicating that one cell three times per
arm showed the ordinary arm **forking between two solution strategies** — a
minimal one-byte repair ($11.78) or an unrequested redesign ($44.27, $41.35),
one of which two graders scored at or below the correctness gate floor — while
FCDD produced the minimal repair in all three runs (spread 1.60×, CV 0.28).
That is the shape the method predicts, on one defect, at a replication depth
where no dispersion statistic can reach significance (Fisher exact on
strategy-by-arm *p* = 0.40; the best attainable *p* in a 3-vs-3 design is
0.10). We therefore report predictability as a **directional observation and a
hypothesis for future work, not a finding**. The blinded-in-name-only quality
comparison is likewise not powered: the paired composite difference is +0.17
with a 95% CI of [−0.44, +0.78], spanning zero.

The honest summary of the trade is asymmetric: **the price is demonstrated;
the product is not yet.** We also report three measurement findings we regard
as more transferable than either ratio: an instruction to review "until no
findings remain" has no fixed point; measured cost covaries with review-loop
length at least as strongly as with method; and processes killed by
infrastructure emit no cost record, in one cell under-counting by ~20×.

**Keywords:** formal methods; empirical software engineering; predictability;
LLM agents; cost measurement; code review; verification

## 1. Introduction

Agentic AI code repair has a predictability problem before it has a cost
problem. An unconstrained agent asked to fix a fault may fix it minimally, or
redesign the subsystem around it, or "fix" code that was never broken; runs of
the same task have been measured to differ by up to 30× in token consumption
[BAI2026], and agent-reported success diverges widely from human-confirmed
success [RONDON2025]. For a maintainer, the expected cost of a fix matters
less than the variance of what they will receive.

Formal Contract-Driven Development (FCDD) is a response to that problem, not
to the cost problem. It surrounds the agent with a machine-checked contract —
a kernel-checked specification the fix must name, satisfy and extend — on the
theory that a repair pinned to named clauses cannot silently become a
redesign, and a change with no clause behind it is visible as such. That is
the design intent. Whether the pinning *works* is an empirical question, and
it is the first of the two questions this study asks:

- **Goal 1 — predictability.** Does the contract make the agent's repairs
  land in a narrower band — of solution strategy, of quality, of cost — and
  does it reduce unfounded changes?
- **Goal 2 — cost.** What does the pinning cost, per defect, against a
  competent ordinary alternative doing the identical repair to the identical
  gate?

The intended thesis, stated before the data so the reader can watch it take
damage: *predictability is a trade-off purchased with elevated cost* — the
ordinary approach is cheap when the problem is easy but unpredictable and
occasionally very expensive, whereas the formal approach costs more up front
and lands in a narrow band. Section 5 tests each half separately. The cost
half survives intact. The predictability half survives only as a directional
observation on one replicated defect, and we say so plainly: this experiment
was powered to price the method, not to prove its purpose.

A second contribution is methodological. Three times a striking result proved
to be an artefact of our apparatus rather than a property of the methods; a
fourth and fifth were caught only by adversarial review of the finished draft,
and this reframing pass added a sixth (a published regression that did not
reproduce; §4.3). Each correction moved a headline, and two reversed its
direction. Section 4 is therefore placed before the results.

## 2. The method under test

### 2.1 The two arms

**Ordinary development-plus-review (Arm A).** Reproduce the symptom, fix the
fault, add a test, obtain a review from a fresh reviewer, address its
findings; stop when the pre-registered gate passes. No formal-methods
vocabulary appears in the instructions.

**FCDD (Arm B).** The same bug report and gate, but the session works around a
pre-built machine-checked contract, following the lifecycle in Figure 1.

![Figure 1 — FCDD as executed in this study: the build-once contract package
(top), the per-defect FCDD repair pipeline it constrains (middle), and the
ordinary arm's pipeline (bottom). Both lanes end at the same sealed
acceptance gate.](figures/fig1_fcdd_process.png)

### 2.2 The four beats and their artefacts

FCDD builds the contract once, in four beats, each of which leaves a distinct
artefact (Figure 1, top band). In this study the build cost $21.75, metered
separately as Arm B's upfront investment.

1. **Prove.** The safety properties are written in prose, then formalised as
   the *spec of record* in a kernel-checked prover — here Lean 4 core,
   producing `Contract.lean`: 1,271 lines, 95 theorems, zero `sorry`, empty
   axiom profile. The completeness gate is that the prover's own accounting
   reports no unproven obligation of any kind.
2. **Twin.** A *pure twin implementation* — side-effect-free, no I/O, no
   clocks — transcribed clause-for-clause from the spec, so that "does the
   code match the spec?" becomes a question a machine can sample.
3. **Bridge.** A *conformance suite* connecting twin to spec and to the real
   engine through the emulator: known witnesses must conform, inputs that
   violate a theorem must fail the *right* clause, and single-field mutations
   must trip exactly their clause. The bridge **samples** agreement; it is not
   a refinement proof, and the method says so.
4. **Attack.** Multi-agent adversarial review with distinct lenses (spec
   adequacy, twin fidelity, integration), findings verified by execution and
   fixed at the layer that caused them.

**What tier of verification this actually is.** All 95 theorems are
discharged by `rfl` — kernel evaluation of a closed decidable Boolean on a
concrete witness. There is no universally-quantified theorem, no induction
and no invariant proof in the contract. The tier is therefore
**kernel-evaluated finite-scope checking**: the Lean kernel used as a
trustworthy test runner. The step-1 package's own summary is scrupulous about
this (it labels the result "small-scope, not a general theorem"); earlier
drafts of this paper were not, and a reader could have taken "95
machine-checked theorems" for deductive proof. The consequence favours the
paper's own cost headline: **we priced the cheapest formal tier, so our cost
ratio is a lower bound** on what deductive verification would have cost. The
artefact belongs with lightweight formal methods rather than with seL4-style
proof.

### 2.3 How the contract constrains the repair agent

The per-defect FCDD session (Figure 1, middle lane) receives the same
symptom-level bug report as Arm A, plus the contract package on disk. Its
instructions require it to: run the bridge against the faulty build and
**locate the violated clause**; if no clause is violated, **extend the
contract** until one is (every one of the seven cells did extend it — by +12
to +35 theorems; §3.2); fix at the offending layer; **re-prove** (the kernel
gate must return green over all theorems, old and new, with zero `sorry`);
re-run the **bridge** on the fixed build; and submit to an **attack round**
of independent reviewers, capped at three rounds like Arm A's review.

This is the mechanism by which predictability *could* arise, stated here as
design rather than result: a redesign must leave every named theorem green,
which prices deviation from specified behaviour; a change with no clause
behind it is structurally visible; and the seeded fault itself becomes a
permanent negative test (its detector must fire by name). Whether any of that
shows up in the data is §5.2 and §5.3's question.

### 2.4 What the method itself disclaims

FCDD's own documentation states two residuals that bound everything this
study can conclude: the contract establishes **coherence, never correctness
of intent** — garbage clauses, kernel-checked, are still garbage — and the
bridge cannot catch a **common-mode error** in which specification and twin
share one author's misconception. Both residuals became measurable events in
this study (§3.2's vacuous antisymmetry clause; §5.4's gate common-mode), so
they are not hypothetical.

## 2.5 Related work

**Empirical cost of formal methods.** The field's own guidance notes that
empirical studies of formal methods are rare [TERBEEK2022]. What exists is
mostly retrospective single-project accounting with no control arm: seL4
needed 2.2 person-years to build against about 20 to verify [STAPLES2014,
KLEIN2009] — though that ~9× bundles roughly 9 person-years of *reusable*
framework and tooling; the seL4-specific proof effort was 11 person-years
(~5×), and Klein et al. expect a repeat verification to take ~6
[KLEIN2009]; ShardStore's authors report
their verification artefacts as 13 % of the codebase and compare that to
"3–10× overhead" figures *drawn from other papers* [BORNHOLT2021]; AWS's TLA+
experience reports rest on counterfactual testimony about what engineers
would otherwise have done [NEWCOMBE2015]; CompCert and Tokeneer report cost
without comparison [LEROY2009, WOODCOCK2009]. The largest survey concedes the
gap directly: "in a majority of projects surveyed, there is no data available
on relative time and costs" [WOODCOCK2009]. Practitioner surveys measure
perception rather than cost [GARAVEL2020, GLEIRSCHER2020]. Pfleeger and
Hatton found "no quantitative evidence that formal design techniques produced
code of higher quality than informally-designed code", but their overall
assessment is mixed rather than negative — they conclude formal specification
"can lead to high-quality code". The study is observational, has no control,
and reports no cost or effort figures [PFLEEGER1997].

**The closest precedent, and what it was.** Sobel and Clarkson compared
formal and informal development across student teams [SOBEL2002]. We
initially credited this as the first *controlled* comparison and disclaimed
priority accordingly. On checking the record that concession was too generous
to the precedent: Berry and Tichy characterise it as a **quasi experiment**
in which "assignment was voluntary and not random", a "Nonequivalent
Posttest-Only Design", and call for "a complete redesign of the experiment"
[BERRY2003]; the analysed sample was 6 formal and 11 control teams, not
twenty. We therefore state the position precisely: theirs is the closest
prior comparison of formal against informal development, it was not
randomised, and it priced neither arm; the authors' rebuttal argues theirs
was field rather than laboratory research [SOBEL2003]. Our sealed acceptance
tests and order-counterbalanced grading are a direct response to that
critique. The closest work in *unit of analysis* is Amusuo et al., who report
87 minutes to develop and 61 to execute each of 73 unit proofs against
recreated defects [AMUSUO2025] — per-artifact cost, but against a
detection-rate baseline rather than a competing method, and in human minutes
rather than tokens.

**Agentic repair, its cost, and its variance.** LLM agents repairing real
defects is established ground [JIMENEZ2024, YANG2024, XIA2023], as is the
finding that simpler, more structured pipelines can beat autonomous agents on
both quality and dollars — Agentless reports $0.70 per issue [XIA2024]. Our
contribution is therefore not "structure helps" but *which* structure, and at
what price. Cost-aware agent evaluation is likewise not ours: Kapoor et al.
argued for cost as a co-equal axis [KAPOOR2024], and Bai et al. characterise
per-task token consumption in far more detail than a two-arm study can,
reporting that runs of the same task can differ by up to 30× [BAI2026] — a
noise floor any cost delta must clear, the documented form of the
unpredictability Goal 1 targets, and the reason we report run counts and
modes explicitly. Benchmark contamination motivated our decision to seed our
own faults rather than use a public leaderboard [ALEITHAN2024], and agent
repair evaluated inside Google shows a large gap between machine-reported and
human-reported defects [RONDON2025]. The nearest neighbour to our design
varies one prompt line controlling specification grounding for +38 percentage
points of correct code [HAERI2026], but reports no token-cost accounting.

**Review effectiveness — where our §4 results are replication, not
discovery.** That code review surfaces far more maintainability issues than
defects is long established. Mäntylä and Lassenius report that **75 % of
defects found in review do not affect visible functionality**; the residual
is not simply "functional" — pooled across their two studies, functional
defects are 17.4 % and false positives 7.5–9.4 % [MANTYLA2009]. Beller et al.
replicate the ratio in open source and add that 7–35 % of review
*suggestions* produce no code change [BELLER2014], anticipated by the
expectation-versus-outcome gap [BACCHELLI2013]. Porter et al. found that team
size and session count did **not** significantly move the defect detection
rate (p = 0.61 and p = 0.51), though they did affect inspection interval
[PORTER1998]. The per-review signal-to-noise figures we match — a median of 3
true defects against 4 false positives and 13 soft maintenance issues — are
**Rigby and Bird's derivation from that Lucent data**, not Porter's own
reported statistics [RIGBY2013]. Rigby and Bird state it plainly from
industrial data: with more reviewers "there was only a minimal increase in
the number of comments … and there was no increase in the number of change
sets submitted (i.e., the same number of 'rounds of reviewing')" [RIGBY2013].
**Our 85 findings without a single defect in the fix is therefore a
replication in a new setting.** What appears unclaimed is the *stopping
rule*: Sadowski et al. put over 80 % of human changes at one review iteration
or fewer [SADOWSKI2018], so twelve rounds under an unbounded instruction is
an order-of-magnitude departure from the human baseline, and we found no
published study of round-count divergence under such a rule.

**Seeded faults — a threat we inherit.** Mutants couple to 73 % of real
faults; of the remaining 27 %, about 10 % would need a new or stronger
operator and **17 % are coupled to no mutant at all** — the two figures
partition the uncoupled 27 % [JUST2014]; correlation with real-fault
detection weakens once suite size is controlled [PAPADAKIS2018]; uncontrolled
mutation-based designs incur Type I errors around 62 % of the time
[PAPADAKIS2016]; and hand-seeded faults tend to be *harder* to detect than
real ones [ANDREWS2005], which matters because ours are agent-seeded. Our
best defence is that single-statement bugs are an abundant, measured class in
real code [KARAMPATSIS2020]. Defects4J [JUST2014B] is the real-fault
benchmark we did not have for Z80 assembly.

**Position bias in LLM grading — a known failure we designed against but did
not observe.** That LLM judges exhibit position bias was reported by Zheng et
al. [ZHENG2023], demonstrated as rank-hacking by reordering [WANG2024], and
shown systematic across 150,000 instances — and strongest **when the quality
gap is small** [SHI2025], which is exactly the regime two competent repair
arms produce. Mitigations include calibration across orders [WANG2024] and
split-and-merge [LI2024]. Our first grading round showed 4 of 4 pairs
favouring the second-presented submission and we diagnosed position bias.
**On re-examination that inference was never supported.** The four round-1
verdicts agree with the counterbalanced round on all four overlapping pairs,
and the observed pattern is fully explained by a genuine arm effect combined
with the pre-registered parity alternation, which happened to place FCDD
second in three of the four graded pairs. The honest statement is that **our
first design could not have detected position bias**, not that we reproduced
it. We retain the counterbalanced design because it removes the confound we
could not otherwise rule out. The instrument's SE-specific validation is
partial, and the unvalidated part cuts against one of our own outputs:
Wang et al. validate LLM-as-judge for **output-based direct scoring** on
code translation and generation, but conclude that current methods "fail
to deliver satisfactory and consistent comparison performance on SE tasks"
under **pairwise comparison**, recommending that "individual scoring should
be preferred over pairwise comparison" [WANGJ2025]. Our per-axis 1–5 rubric
falls on the validated side of that line; the holistic pairwise verdict
(`which_is_better_overall`, §5.3) falls on the side the source argues
against — a source-backed reason, beyond its non-registration, to weight
the pre-registered axes over that verdict, as §5.3 already does.

**Method comparison and effort.** For controlled technique experiments we
follow standard guidance [KO2015, SJOBERG2005, FEITELSON2022]. The closest
analogue to our central awkwardness is the pair-programming meta-analysis: a
technique that improved quality while costing significantly more effort,
where the accepted response is to report effort as a *separate dependent
variable* rather than equalise it [HANNAY2009]. We adopt that framing for
token cost.

**What remains ours.** Searching specifically for prior work pricing a
formal-methods workflow in LLM token cost against a conventional one returned
nothing we could verify. We state that as a searched-and-not-found result.
The defensible contribution is the conjunction: a concurrently-run control
arm on the same faults, a directly metered cost unit, a repeated-measures
design that project-level accounting cannot provide, pre-committed outcome
measurement, and the stopping-rule and strategy-bimodality results.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 System, faults, execution

The subject is a chess engine of 7,343 lines of hand-written Z80 assembly:
alpha-beta search, transposition tables, quiescence, Zobrist hashing,
built-in perft. An independent agent seeded seven single-fault variants, each
differing from pristine by **exactly one byte** in the assembled binary,
wrote symptom-level reports with no location hints, and sealed an answer key
whose hash was published before any repair began.

Each arm×defect cell ran as an isolated headless session on the same model at
the same effort, with no network access.

**Which version of the method was measured.** Arm B loaded the method at run
time — its prompt directs the agent to invoke the `formal-contract-dev` skill
before working — so the arm is bound to whatever that document said during the
runs. That document is mutable and has since changed. The version measured here
is archived in this repository at `skills/formal-contract-dev/SKILL.md`
(commit `4ca5bb3`, 2026-08-04; 436 lines; sha256 `68e384df562bad87…`), and every
number in this paper refers to it. The installed copy has since gained one
additional item (16.5, a solver/reviewer serialisation turnstile, added
2026-08-07 in response to an unrelated incident); the change is purely
additive, so re-running Arm B today would follow strictly more process than we
measured, which can only raise its cost. We note the irony that the method
under test has just acquired a rule about freezing shared mutable state so a
reviewer is not measuring a moving target, while the experiment measuring it
was reading that method from a mutable path. FCDD's contract was built once as a
separately measured phase — 1,271 lines of specification, 95 machine-checked
theorems, empty axiom profile, twin, bridge, solver tier — at **$21.75**.

### 3.2 An asymmetry we did not design and must disclose

The contract was built by reading the **pristine** engine, so Arm B held a
derived oracle for correct behaviour that Arm A never received; Arm A's only
prior artefact was the symptom report. That asymmetry is real and we do not
minimise it — it recurs in the interpretation of every Goal-1 result below,
because a "consistent" repair guided by a pristine-derived oracle may be
consistency purchased by the oracle, not by the method's structure.

**But an earlier draft's claim that the contract "covers 7/7 faults by
construction" is false, and we withdraw it.** Diffing each cell's
specification against the 95-theorem baseline (re-counted mechanically for
this draft; the counts match) shows every Arm B cell had to *extend* the
contract: bug07 +12, bug02 +16, bug05 +18, bug01 +29, bug06 +29, bug04 +33,
bug03 +35 theorems. The rule-layer clauses C1–C14 covered the rule-layer
faults; the **search and evaluation layer was not covered** and had to be
specified from scratch.

One case is worth reporting in its own right. The baseline contained an
evaluation-antisymmetry property, `evalWhite(mirror p) = -evalWhite p`. A
**global sign inversion satisfies that identity exactly** — so the
pristine-derived contract was *vacuous* against bug04's entire fault class,
and the arm had to discover this and add absolute-orientation anchors. That
is a too-weak-specification result obtained under controlled conditions, and
it is evidence against the very oracle advantage we are disclosing.

Two weaker mechanism statements survive re-derivation (an earlier draft's
grouping — "the four cells needing new properties median \$49.16 against
\$24.19" — could not be reproduced from the deposited artefacts and is
withdrawn; Appendix B #11). First, the raw number of theorems a cell added
does **not** predict its cost (Spearman ρ = 0.03 across the seven cells —
bug03 added the most theorems and was nearly the cheapest). Second, the three
faults seeded in the search/evaluation layer, which the baseline contract did
not cover, have a median cost of \$49.63 against \$27.62 for the four
rule-layer faults. We report that as a mechanism consistent with
coverage-driven cost, not a tested effect (n = 7, and one rule-layer cell —
bug02 at \$50.37 — sits in the wrong group).

An earlier draft asserted that cells had "no path to the pristine sources".
That is true of each cell's filesystem and false in substance. The fair
design would have given Arm A a cost-matched upfront artefact — roughly
$21.75 of characterisation tests built once from the pristine engine — and we
did not. This advantages FCDD on localisation, and it would not transfer to
faults of omission, which lie outside any contract by definition.

### 3.3 Measurement

Cost is reported three ways, because they disagree and the disagreement
matters: the platform's own figure; the same token counts priced with our
pre-registered rate table; and raw token totals. **Token totals come from
each session's `modelUsage`, which includes reviewer subagents.** An earlier
ledger recorded the main-loop `usage` field only, under-counting 22 of the
deposited rows by factors of 1.1× to 82× — a defect that falsified our own
claim that the deposited token counters were ground truth. The published
ledger has been rebuilt from `modelUsage` (`tools/fix_ledger_tokens.py`,
idempotent); each corrected row retains its original value in a
`tokens_source` column rather than being silently overwritten.

Reviewer invocations are counted mechanically from each session's transcript,
because (§4.3) this covaries with cost as strongly as method does and is not
a quantity a session can self-report away.

### 3.4 Pre-registration and its breaches

The protocol was frozen before any run; changes are dated amendments
(A1–A10). We record two breaches:

- **Rule 5 (replication) was honoured late.** It requires re-running any pair
  whose arm gap is under 30% of the larger cost. bug01's gap is 10.8% and it
  is the only cell that triggers the rule. The v4 draft went to review with
  the rule undischarged; the replication has since been completed (three runs
  per arm) and §5.2 reports it.
- **The metric was substituted without amendment.** D3 froze a token-weighted
  price table and stated that an unpriced model would be "raw-only". The
  model used is unpriced there; we reported platform dollars. Reverse-
  engineering the platform's rates from 41 runs shows they match D3's rates
  within ~6%, so the substitution is materially harmless — but it was
  undocumented, in a paper whose §4.4 argues that platform self-reports must
  not be used unreconciled.

## 4. Instrument findings

### 4.1 Reviews that never closed
Subagents defaulted to background execution and a headless session ends when
its main loop ends, so sessions terminated with reviews pending; five cells
self-reported waiting for reviewers. The stage was under-executed and
under-counted, symmetrically across arms.

### 4.2 Unbounded review has no fixed point
The corrected instruction read *iterate until the reviewer has no findings
left*. A competent reviewer examining any test suite can always name one more
uncovered case, so the rule has no fixed point.

In one cell Arm A fixed the seeded fault correctly on its first pass (one
byte, `cp WN` → `cp WP`), then ran **12 review rounds producing 85 findings,
none of which was a defect in the fix**. Findings per round: 7, 7, 5, 11, 8,
9, 5, 5, 5, 3, 8, 12 — OLS slope +0.025 findings/round, *R²* = 0.001. No
convergence.

**Correction to an earlier draft:** we wrote that we halted this run. The
record shows `api_error` 429 — *the session quota killed it* — and a second
attempt of $67.94 that our accounting missed. Summed per our own rule, the
cell cost **at least $128.62** across 7.7 hours, and the bounded re-run cost
$7.32: a reduction of **at least 17.6×**, not the 8.3× first reported. The
finding survives; our framing had claimed a judgement call where the record
shows an infrastructure kill.

### 4.3 Cost covaries with review effort at least as strongly as with method

An early draft claimed cost "tracked reviewer count more tightly than which
arm was running", from five cells in which arm and count were collinear. That
is not identifiable on those data, and the ordering flips on a bookkeeping
choice.

**The regression published in draft v4 is itself corrected here.** v4 printed
`cost = 4.13 + 21.35·ArmB + 7.13·reviewers, R² = 0.826` with arm-alone
*R²* = 0.644 and reviewers-alone *R²* = 0.583. Re-deriving from the deposited
table for this revision (13 final-instrument cells with a deposited platform
cost; `tools/analyse_predictability.py`) gives different coefficients, and we
could not construct any subset of the deposited data that reproduces the v4
numbers. The corrected fit:

> cost = 1.56 + 16.85·ArmB + 8.76·reviewers, *R²* = 0.873.
> Arm alone *R²* = 0.580 (gap $27.90); reviewers alone *R²* = 0.711.
> Controlling for reviewer count attenuates the arm gap from $27.90 to
> $16.85 — roughly **40% of the measured method difference is attributable to
> Arm B invoking 1.8× as many reviewers** (mean 2.83 vs 1.57). Figure 5.

The correction *strengthens* the section's claim — reviewers alone now
explain more variance than arm alone — but that is luck, not vindication: a
published regression failed to reproduce and we cannot reconstruct where its
coefficients came from (Appendix B #10).

![Figure 5 — Cell cost against reviewer invocations, 13 final-instrument
cells (the imputed bug03/B cell is shown hollow and excluded from the
fit).](figures/fig5_review_effort_vs_cost.png)

The reviewer-count asymmetry is a design defect: our bounded rule caps review
*rounds* at three, but FCDD's attack beat is multi-reviewer per round, so the
rule granted Arm B up to five invocations and Arm A at most three. It is
symmetric in form and asymmetric in effect, in both directions — inflating
Arm B's cost and plausibly driving its test-quality score.

A further correction, retained from v4: we once wrote that bounding review
compressed Arm A's range "to $7–$16". Arm A's final-instrument costs are
$7.11–$44.27. Six of seven cells fall in $7–$17; the seventh is bug01 at
$44.27 with three reviewers against $8.66 with one. **Bounding rounds did not
bound reviewer invocations, and cost followed the latter.**

### 4.4 Killed processes emit no cost record
One cell reported $1.06; its first attempt had been killed without emitting a
result record. Recovering 34.67M tokens from the transcripts and pricing them
with our pre-registered table gives **$23.98** (defensible band $23–$33). An
earlier draft reported $45.76 by borrowing a blended rate from another cell
whose own denominator was truncated — inflating it ~1.6×. The under-count is
therefore ~20×, not 43×.

A second failure mode appears in our own ledger and we had missed it: one
record carries a cost of $49.19 with **all four token counters at zero**.
Exit reports can be internally self-contradictory, not merely absent.

### 4.5 Blinding defects in grading
Our first grading round was voided: submissions were diffed against pristine,
but a correct minimal repair *restores* pristine, so the repair was
invisible; FCDD's verification artefacts live outside the variant directory
and were omitted, so FCDD scored 1/5 for testing on the grounds that it
shipped no tests; fixing that leaked paths naming the arm.

**The regraded round is not blinded, and we now quantify that rather than
hedging it.** Counting treatment-signature terms (theorem, specification,
clause, solver, witness, mutant) in one packet's two submissions, at
comparable length: **0 occurrences for the ordinary arm, 37 for FCDD**. That
is not a residue that survived scrubbing; it is a label. Every grader could
identify the treatment with certainty. Consequently **"blinded grading" must
be withdrawn as a competing-interest mitigation** — it did not occur. The
accurate description of §5.3 is: *unblinded graders preferred the submission
that visibly contains the treatment.* Compounding this, on three of seven
defects the source fixes were byte-identical, so the verdict turned entirely
on the visible artefact, and the rubric's test-quality axis mechanically
rewards shipping more verification material — which is FCDD's definition.

data** — *N* = upfront ÷ per-defect saving is undefined when the saving is
negative on every pair. Note that our design cannot test the *learning* half
of the hypothesis at all: cells are memoryless by construction, so per-defect
cost cannot fall with experience.

### 5.2 Predictability (Goal 1a): dispersion, and what replication showed

This is the section the method exists to win, so we hold it to the same
standard the cost section survived.

**Across defects, the contrast rests on one cell.** With all seven cells,
ordinary development's cost spread is 6.23× (CV 0.86) against FCDD's 2.31×
(CV 0.33) — the shape the thesis predicts. Excluding bug01, the spreads are
**identical at 2.31×** (CVs 0.34 and 0.36). A predictability claim whose
entire evidentiary weight sits in one of seven cells is not a claim about
the method; it is a claim about that cell (Figure 3a).

**Within the one replicated cell, the arms differ in kind, not just spread.**
Our pre-registered rule 5 required replicating bug01 (arm gap 10.8%, under
the 30% trigger); we ran three runs per arm under the final instrument
(Figure 3b). Arm A produced two different solutions: a **minimal one-byte
repair** restoring the pristine binary ($11.78), or an unrequested **redesign
of the quiescence policy** ($44.27, $41.35) — the same redesign two graders
scored at or below the correctness gate floor for an arithmetic underflow and
a search-wide blast radius, and which the sealed acceptance script passed
identically. Arm B produced the minimal, byte-identical repair in **all three
runs** ($49.63, $79.28, $51.40; spread 1.60×, CV 0.28). Arm A's cost is not
scattered by noise; it is determined by which strategy the run converges on
(within-mode spreads 1.07× and — including the instrument-v3 run, whose cost
is non-comparable but whose strategy datum is — 1.36×; between modes 3.8×).
This is a distinct phenomenon from the ~30× stochastic token variance
reported for agentic coding tasks [BAI2026]: it is bimodal strategy
selection, which is interpretable rather than noise.

![Figure 3 — (a) Per-defect costs by arm; the bug01 cell (hollow) carries the
entire across-defect dispersion contrast. (b) The bug01 replication: three
runs per arm; marker shape is the solution strategy the run converged
on.](figures/fig3_dispersion.png)

**What this supports, stated exactly.** The strategy table (Arm A: 1 minimal
/ 2 redesign; Arm B: 3 / 0) gives Fisher exact *p* = 0.40 (0.43 including the
v3 strategy datum). At three runs per arm, **no dispersion statistic can
reach significance** — the best attainable two-sided *p* in any 3-vs-3
permutation test is 0.10. And the oracle asymmetry of §3.2 offers a rival
explanation that requires no method effect at all: an arm holding a
pristine-derived executable model may converge on the pristine-restoring
repair *because it holds the model*, not because a contract disciplines its
strategy. The honest classification is therefore: **a directional
observation, consistent with the method's design intent, on one defect,
confounded by the oracle, and untestable at this replication depth.** It is
the right hypothesis for the next experiment (§8), and it is not a finding
of this one.

**Solution-matched cost, reported but not as an estimate.** Restricting to
runs where both arms produced the byte-identical repair leaves three Arm B
runs against one Arm A run ($11.78). The three ratios are 4.21×, 4.36× and
6.73×. We quote them individually because a denominator of one is not an
estimator. What survives is qualitative: on this defect, when both arms
produced the same repair, FCDD cost several times more — and the 1.12× in
the §5.1 table, the study's narrowest ratio, compares FCDD's minimal fix
against Arm A's expensive redesign. Matched on solution it is closer to the
study's widest.

**What the cost data adds to Goal 1.** If "predictable" includes "you can
budget for it", the seven-pair table carries one more observation: Arm A's
per-defect cost has a long right tail (six cells in $7–$17, one at $44), and
§4.2 shows what the tail looks like without a bounded stopping rule — at
least $128.62 on a defect whose fix was one byte and landed in the first
pass. FCDD's band is narrower relative to its own scale but sits three to
five times higher. Neither arm's *within-defect* cost variance is
characterised beyond the one replicated cell, where Arm B's three runs span
1.60× — with one run inflated by resumption overhead after an infrastructure
kill — against Arm A's strategy-driven 3.76×.

### 5.3 Quality and unfounded changes (Goal 1b) — re-measured under a counterbalanced design

The earlier comparison was withdrawn because graders preferred the
second-presented submission in 4 of 4 pairs under a 3:1 order imbalance. We
re-ran the whole grading with **each of the seven pairs graded twice, once in
each presentation order, by fourteen independent graders**.

Presentation preference came out **exactly 7 first / 7 second**, so order is
neutralised, and **all seven pairs returned the same verdict in both
orders** — evidence of grader reliability the single-rater design could not
provide.

**On the graders' holistic preference, FCDD was favoured on 6 of 7 pairs**
(*p* = 0.125). The paired composite difference is **+0.17 with a 95% CI of
[−0.44, +0.78] — the interval spans zero**, and the study is not powered to
make a quality claim (Figure 4). Two further caveats make even the
directional reading unsafe. First, `which_is_better_overall` was **never
pre-registered** — the protocol registers only the three 1–5 axes and the
no-axis-below-3 gate — and it contradicts the graders' own rubric totals on
three of seven pairs, all three toward FCDD. Scored on the pre-registered
instrument the pairs split 3 / 2 with 2 ties, *p* = 1.00. Second, the
aggregate is carried entirely by one pair: **excluding bug01, the arms tie at
exactly 4.50 / 4.50 overall and correctness risk reverses to 4.67 (ordinary)
against 4.42 (FCDD)**. The only axis that survives exclusion is test
quality — and that axis mechanically rewards shipping more verification
material, which is the treatment's definition (§4.5).

The advantage is concentrated in test quality and partly returned in clarity;
graders repeatedly found FCDD's evidence stronger but harder to read. On
three of the seven defects the two source fixes were **byte-for-byte
identical**, so the verdict turned entirely on verification. The defensible
claim is therefore narrow: FCDD did not produce better *fixes*; it produced
better *evidence that the fix is right and will stay right* — seeding each
fault as a permanent negative test, proving its detector fires by name, and
in three cases closing a **stale-evidence false-green** in which the gate had
been passing while the build's own self-test failed.

**What this measures against the hallucination-reduction goal.** Goal 1
claims the contract suppresses unfounded changes. The graded record contains
exactly one clear instance of the failure mode the goal targets: Arm A's
bug01 redesign — an unrequested rework of the quiescence policy that
introduced an arithmetic-underflow risk, **scored 2 on correctness risk,
below the pre-registered gate floor of 3, and passed the sealed acceptance
script anyway**. FCDD produced no graded submission below the floor, and on
the replicated defect it never produced the redesign. That is the design
intent made visible — once. Against it: both arms produced beyond-minimal
changes on other defects (Arm A's bug06 extra was a *genuine latent-defect
fix* the grader valued, so "more than asked" is not per se a defect; Arm B's
bug06 binary also differs from pristine), the quality interval spans zero,
and the one clean instance sits on the same oracle-confounded cell as every
other Goal-1 signal. **This experiment therefore does not establish
hallucination reduction.** What it establishes is narrower and still worth
having: the sealed symptom-level gate — the industry's default arbiter —
**cannot see the difference** between a careful repair and a risky redesign,
because both pass it. A method that wants credit for suppressing unfounded
changes must first deploy an instrument able to detect them; the contract's
clause-level diff is such an instrument, and the acceptance script is not.

### 5.4 Correctness and beyond-gate findings

Every defect was repaired correctly by both arms under the final instrument,
confirmed by sealed acceptance scripts. This is weaker evidence than it
sounds: the scripts were written by the agent that injected the faults, from
the same understanding of the engine's semantics, so a shared misconception
is undetectable — the identical common-mode residual the paper quotes for
FCDD and had not applied to its own instrument.

Both arms produced findings beyond the reported defect. FCDD produced them
systematically, including a real defect in its own delivered package (a gate
measured against a stale binary). Ordinary review found a latent
transposition-table defect and an unadjudicated terminal-position path — and
the blinded grader judged *that* extra the riskier of two on the same defect.
"Finds more than it was asked to" is not FCDD-specific.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Goal 1: predictability — designed for, not demonstrated

The method's purpose receives, from this experiment, the verdict *consistent
but unproven*. Every Goal-1 signal points the same way — the across-defect
spread, the single-mode replication, the absence of any FCDD submission below
the gate floor, the graders' 6-of-7 preference — and not one of them survives
its own caveat: the spread contrast lives in one cell; the replication depth
caps attainable significance at *p* = 0.10; the gate-floor instance is a
single event; the preference interval spans zero; and the oracle asymmetry
(§3.2) predicts most of it with no method effect at all. If we had led with
Goal 1 and reported these as findings, we would be doing with dispersions
exactly what we criticise cost claims for doing with anecdotes.

What the study *does* contribute to Goal 1 is sharper than a null: it
identifies the phenomenon worth measuring (bimodal strategy selection, not
continuous cost noise), the instrument capable of measuring it (solution-mode
coding by binary diff against pristine — mechanical and cheap), the confound
that must be removed (give the control arm a cost-matched pristine-derived
artefact), and the replication depth required (at k = 3 the best attainable
*p* is 0.10; k ≥ 6 per arm per defect is the floor for a meaningful test).
Section 8 turns that into a design.

### 6.2 Goal 2: cost — demonstrated, with one imputation caveat

FCDD was more expensive on every one of seven matched faults, holding a free
pristine-derived oracle its competitor lacked, and it never amortised. The
direction survives every cost basis and every sensitivity we ran; the
significance (p = 0.016 vs 0.125) rests on one transcript-recovery imputation
we performed on our own study. The overhead on this workload was three to
five times, not the order of magnitude folklore sometimes suggests — and not
the near-zero that would make the method free to adopt. Since the contract
never amortised, the trade the thesis proposes is real only if Goal 1's
product exists; this study priced the premium without confirming the
insurance pays out.

### 6.3 The measurement lessons

The result most likely to transfer is neither ratio. It is that this study
produced six confident, plausible, wrong findings, and that every one was
caught by a mechanical check independent of the agent that produced it —
three by our own gates and transcripts, two by adversarial review of the
finished draft, and one (the §4.3 regression) by the re-derivation discipline
this revision imposed. Several of the errors favoured the conclusion we were
drifting toward.

For the growing practice of measuring agent-based development, the concrete
lessons are: reconcile self-reported cost against transcripts, because exit
reports can be absent, truncated or internally contradictory; report reviewer
invocation counts, because they covary with cost at least as strongly as
method does; control the review termination rule, because it moved one cell
17.6×; randomise presentation order in grading, because our first design
could not have detected the bias it needed to exclude; and re-derive every
published number from the deposited artefacts before each revision, because
one of ours could not be.

### 6.4 An illustrative parallel: the same model, with and without a solver

A side experiment run on unrelated infrastructure offers a parallel worth
stating, with the caveat that it is an illustration and not evidence: it was
not controlled, each cell ran once, and it measures chess rather than repair.

The same model played a five-game ladder against a deterministic 1–5 ply chess
engine, twice: once choosing every move from its own judgement, once with
Stockfish 18 at fixed depth selecting the moves.

| Engine level | judgement only | with Stockfish |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | won, 27 moves | won, 27 moves |
| 2 | won, 53 | won, 26 |
| 3 | **lost** | won, 15 |
| 4 | won, 73 | won, 36 |
| 5 | **lost** | won, 45 |

Unaided: 3–2, and unpredictably so — the two losses were not at the two hardest
levels. Aided: 5–0, with the won games roughly halved in length. The interesting
part is *where* the solver helped. The agents logged their own candidate move
before each engine query. At level 2 the engine twice averted material loss. At
levels 3, 4 and 5 it never rescued anything: the model's own worst candidate was
still winning at every decision point. The solver's contribution was to remove a
tail, not to raise a mean.

That is the same shape this study measured, and it suggests why. **A chess engine
is a generator; a proof kernel is a filter.** Stockfish proposes a better move
than the model would have chosen. Lean proposes nothing — it refuses claims that
do not follow. FCDD therefore leaves the generator untouched and adds a filter,
which predicts exactly what we observed: dispersion narrows (§5.2) while the
paired quality difference stays indistinguishable from zero (§5.3). A filter
removes the catastrophic tail; it does not make the typical repair better. Our
null result on quality is not a failure to detect an effect that ought to be
there — it is what a filter-shaped intervention should produce.

The parallel also marks its own limits, and they cut against the method rather
than for it. Chess supplies a total, cheap, decidable oracle: Stockfish evaluates
*any* legal position, and the objective — win — is given. Design supplies none of
that. A contract constrains only what it states, the statement must be written by
the same fallible agent whose work it is meant to bound, and writing it is where
the cost and the errors concentrate. The seven cells here are the direct
evidence: every one required *extending* the contract by 12 to 35 theorems before
the fault was even expressible (§3.2), and an earlier draft's claim that the
contract covered all seven faults by construction was false and withdrawn
(Appendix B). Stockfish needed no such extension to see any of the 30,000
positions it evaluated.

The honest reading, then, is narrow: where an external, machine-checked oracle
covers the decision, it converts a variable outcome into a bounded one at a
price. Chess is the easy case, because the oracle is total and free. Formal
contracts are the hard case, because the oracle must first be built — and the
paper's headline cost is largely the bill for building it.

## 7. Threats to validity

1. **Sample size.** Seven defects, k = 1 except one cell at k = 3, one
   codebase, one language, one model.
2. **The oracle asymmetry** of §3.2 — FCDD received a pristine-derived
   executable model; Arm A received nothing comparable. It did not cover the
   faults by construction (every cell extended it), but it is the most
   consequential design flaw, and it supplies a method-free explanation for
   every Goal-1 signal.
3. **Instrument instability.** The prompt changed four times; only
   final-version cells are compared. Version purity was achieved;
   *reproducibility was not demonstrated* — the one replicated cell spans
   3.8× across runs within the same arm and instrument.
4. **Interruption asymmetry.** Counted from the deposited attempt records:
   **6 of 7** Arm B cells and 2 of 7 Arm A cells required a resume. This
   statistic has been published as "every/none", then "5 of 7"; only this
   third value is derived from the ledger. Applying the completing-attempt
   proxy symmetrically gives 1.14×, 1.54×, 3.95×, 4.04× (median 2.75×); it is
   undefined where a completing attempt is a 34-second confirmation. The
   proxy is a floor, not a bracket. One Arm B bug01 replicate ($79.28)
   likewise carries resumption overhead inside the Goal-1 dispersion data.
5. **Review-budget asymmetry.** The bounded rule caps rounds, not
   invocations, granting Arm B 1.8× the review effort (§4.3).
6. **Gate common-mode.** Acceptance scripts were written by the
   fault-injecting agent (§5.4) — and §5.3 shows the gate passing a
   below-floor redesign, so the weakness is demonstrated, not conjectured.
7. **Grading.** Label-blind, not treatment-blind: a submission containing a
   machine-checked specification is identifiable as the formal arm regardless
   of scrubbing (37:0 signature terms; §4.5). Order confounding was removed
   by counterbalancing, but each pair×order was judged once, so we have
   order-consistency, not inter-rater reliability. Graders ran on a different
   model (`claude-fable-5`) from the arms and their reviewers
   (`claude-opus-5` in every final-instrument cell) — an earlier draft
   asserted a shared model family, which is factually wrong for this data.
8. **Population.** Single-byte seeded faults are individually localisable,
   which plausibly favours whichever method is cheaper per fault; and none of
   them is a fault of omission, the class that lies outside any contract.
9. **Target-language externality.** Every cell repaired one language, and
   an unusual one: we assume hand-written Z80 assembly is far sparser in
   LLM training corpora than Python, C, Java or JavaScript. **That premise
   is plausible but unverified** — the training composition of the models
   used here is not public, and neither work cited below measures Z80's
   share (Orlanski et al.'s low-resource set is Dart, Lua, Rust, C#, R,
   Julia and Haskell). What the literature does establish is the axis:
   code-generation performance varies with a language's share of the
   training distribution — multi-language benchmarks were built expressly
   to expose the effect of language frequency [CASSANO2022], and
   rebalancing that distribution helps low-resource languages, though the
   absolute effect is modest: the headline "66.48 % better pass@k" is a
   *relative* improvement, and the underlying absolute pass@100 moves (4B
   model, natural vs. balanced) run from −3.1 points (C#, which
   *regresses*) to +13.0 (Julia), mean ≈ +6 [ORLANSKI2023]. That one
   language moves the wrong way under rebalancing is itself a caution: the
   frequency axis is real but not monotone, so what follows is an
   expectation, not a law. Two effects follow, and their expected signs
   can be stated. *(i) Sparsity widens the unguided arm.* With fewer memorised
   idioms to recall, more of an unconstrained session is search rather than
   recall, so part of Arm A's observed dispersion — and therefore part of
   the Goal-1 contrast — may be a property of Z80, not of the missing
   contract. (The sign holds for within-strategy wander; for strategy
   *forking* it is genuinely ambiguous, since a rich language also offers
   more memorised redesigns to fork to.) *(ii) Closed semantics flatter the
   contract.* Z80's fixed instruction set, absence of runtime and library
   surface, and small machine state make a finite-scope contract unusually
   cheap to build ($21.75) and unusually binding; a language with a large
   runtime would cost more to specify and bind less. Jointly the expected
   direction differs by goal: a mainstream-language replication should
   *raise* the cost ratio — recall cheapens Arm A while specification
   surface raises Arm B's contract side, both pushing the same way, making
   our measured 3–5× a plausible floor, consistent with §2.2's
   cheapest-tier argument — and *shrink* the dispersion contrast (a
   narrower unguided arm against a less binding contract). On language
   grounds, then, the cost finding is conservative and the predictability
   observation is anti-conservative. Effect (ii) also compounds the oracle
   asymmetry (§3.2) rather than sitting orthogonal to it: a
   pristine-derived oracle is worth most exactly where the model's prior
   knowledge is thinnest. None of this is testable here, because the target
   language never varies across any cell. The nearest within-study proxy —
   fault subsystem — is not language variation at all; for the record,
   Arm A's cost spread is 1.68× within the four rule-layer cells against
   3.92× within the three search/eval cells, a contrast carried once again
   by bug01 and confounded with defect difficulty and contract coverage.
   (The secondary reading of "output language" — the natural language of
   prompts and reasoning — was English throughout and identical across
   arms, so it cannot contribute to a between-arm contrast.) §8.2 specifies
   the replication that would make the language axis measurable.
10. **Contamination.** The orchestrator read session self-reports while
    ingesting results and unsealed the key at grading.
11. **Conflict of interest.** See declarations. An external audit found eight
    discretionary choices, seven of which favoured the authors' own method.

## 8. Future work: buying more predictability, and knowing when you have

The question this study leaves is not whether to run it again but what would
make Goal 1 decidable, and what each candidate improvement to the method
would buy per dollar. We order these by how directly they attack the gap
between design intent and demonstrated result. The first two items are
methodological; the rest are changes to FCDD itself.

**8.1 Power the predictability claim before making it.** The phenomenon is
now identified: bimodal *strategy selection*, detectable mechanically (binary
diff against pristine classifies minimal-fix vs redesign at zero marginal
cost). An earlier draft costed this design against an arithmetic error, which
we correct here because it would have bought a null: we claimed a 6/0-vs-3/3
strategy split at k = 6 reaches *p* ≈ 0.01. It does not. That table's exact
two-sided *p* is **0.18** — the hypergeometric support is 20/220, 90/220,
90/220, 20/220, so the two tails sum to 40/220. A design justified by that
number would have been underpowered by roughly an order of magnitude.

The corrected arithmetic. For *perfect* separation (one arm all-minimal, the
other all-redesign), the attainable two-sided floor is 0.10 at k = 3, **0.029
at k = 4**, 0.0079 at k = 5 and 0.0022 at k = 6 — so k = 4 is the smallest
run count that can clear 0.05 at all. But perfect separation is not what this
study observed: bug01's replication gave 3/3 minimal for Arm B against 1
minimal / 2 redesign for Arm A. Powering for that realistic 2:1 forking rate
needs **k = 10** to clear 0.05 (*p* = 0.033) and **k = 12** to reach 0.014 —
not k = 6.

The design implication is not simply "buy more runs". A 2×2 Fisher test on
strategy *labels* discards the magnitude information that motivated the claim
in the first place, which is why it needs so many runs. The primary test
should be a **pre-registered dispersion estimator on the cost data itself**
(log-cost CV, paired across defects, with a permutation test), with the
strategy classification as a mechanistic secondary outcome rather than the
inferential workhorse. Paired across defects, the sign-test floor is 0.031 at
six defects and 0.016 at seven — so *breadth* buys power more cheaply than
depth for the across-defect dispersion claim, while depth (k per cell) is
what buys a within-cell variance estimate. The current study has seven
defects at k = 1 and one defect at k = 3, which is why a single defect can
carry the entire contrast.

A defensible design therefore needs all four of: **breadth** (≥ 7 defects, so
no single cell dominates); **depth** (k ≥ 4 per arm per defect, for a
within-cell variance estimate); a **pre-registered estimator and test** (we
pre-register neither at our peril, having watched estimator choice move our
own cost ratio from 2.8× to 4.3×); and the control arm equipped with a
**cost-matched pristine-derived artefact** (≈$21.75 of characterisation
tests) so the oracle confound of §3.2 is spent symmetrically. One further
lever this study never pulled: our seven faults were deliberately homogeneous
— single-byte, comparable difficulty — and dispersion can only appear where
problems differ enough to fork. Sampling defects across a *deliberate
difficulty range* gives the effect room to exist.

Cost at observed per-cell prices ($15.33 Arm A, $40.47 Arm B, $55.80 per
matched pair): seven defects at k = 4 is 28 pairs ≈ **$1,560**; at k = 6, 42
pairs ≈ **$2,340**. That is one to two times what this entire study cost, for
one decidable bit — and it should be stated in any proposal that promises the
claim more cheaply.

**8.2 A cross-language replication prices the language confound.** The
target-language threat (§7, item 9) is untestable in this design because
the language being repaired never varies, and it bears on the two goals
with opposite signs — it argues our cost ratio is a floor and our
dispersion contrast is inflated. The decisive experiment manipulates
language and holds everything else as fixed as a different codebase
permits: seed the **same seven fault classes** — all of them
language-portable (an inverted guard, an unreset counter, a generation
mask, an evaluation sign, a rights-bookkeeping slip, a cache depth bound, a
terminal misclassification) — into a chess engine of comparable scope in at
least one high-resource language (Python or C; compact open-source engines
exist in both), build the contract fresh for that engine — the build cost
is itself a primary measurement, since effect (ii) predicts it rises with
runtime and library surface — and run both arms under §8.1's controls
(cost-matched control-arm artefact, pre-registered dispersion estimator,
mechanical solution-mode coding). At this study's measured per-cell means
($15.33 Arm A, $40.47 Arm B), one added language costs ≈$390 of runs at
k = 1 and ≈$1,170 at k = 3, plus contract build and seeding — the same
order as §8.1, with which it shares runs if the k ≥ 6 defects are drawn
from the new language. What it can settle: whether the unguided arm's
spread and strategy-forking rate move with training-data share (effect i),
and whether the contract's build and per-defect extension costs track
semantic surface (effect ii) — each a signed prediction stated in §7 before
any data exist. What it cannot: a different engine is a different program,
so language is separated from codebase only up to the fault-class matching;
and it says nothing about faults of omission (§7, item 8) or about other
model families. A third, mid-frequency language turns the two-point
contrast into a trend for roughly another $1,200.

**8.3 Certified extraction: from sampling agreement to correctness by
construction.** Case 01's deepest structural weakness is that spec, twin and
bridge *sample* each other's agreement. The Rocq/Coq route — specify the
contract in Rocq, prove the properties deductively, and **extract executable
OCaml** from the proved specification, as CompCert does at scale [LEROY2009]
— replaces the twin-and-bridge beats with an artefact that is correct by
construction relative to its spec. What it buys for Goal 1 is qualitative:
for covered properties, the failure mode shifts from *detected downstream*
to *unrepresentable*, and the per-defect verification cost for that class
approaches zero — which is precisely the regime in which this study's
never-amortising upfront cost could amortise. The crossover arithmetic that
§5.1 found undefined (negative saving on every pair) becomes well-defined
the moment the marginal cost of one defect class drops below the ordinary
arm's. What it costs: deductive proof effort well above the $21.75
finite-scope tier we priced (that figure is a *floor*, per §2.2); and
extraction certifies the extracted core against *its own spec* only — it
does not certify the spec (bug04's vacuous antisymmetry clause would survive
extraction untouched), the FFI boundary, the numeric-width remapping, or the
parts left in Z80 and JavaScript. Those three leaks are exactly where
"extracted therefore correct" fails in practice, and closing them
(proved input domains, checked arithmetic, differential tests at the
boundary) must be budgeted as first-class work, not a footnote. Honest
framing to preserve: the case must state which fraction of the system the
certified core actually covers.

**8.4 An independent oracle attacks the common-mode residual.** Both the
method's own disclaimer (§2.4) and our gate finding (§5.4) reduce to one
sentence: *spec and checker share an author.* The cheapest structural attack
is a second, independently-authored specification to differential-test
against, and one partially exists: **Chess.lean**
(<https://github.com/dwrensha/Chess.lean>; verified 2026-07-30 and
re-verified for this draft), a Lean 4 formalisation — self-described as
incomplete — of board representation, move legality, turn tracking and
checkmate detection, oriented toward proving mate puzzles rather than
specifying an engine. Its rules/legality/mate layer overlaps our contract's
C1–C6/C11 well; the strategy layer this study's expensive cells needed
(search termination, eval anchoring, TT soundness) is out of its scope.
Highest-value use is therefore **as an independent oracle**: differential-
test our spec's move generation and terminal decisions against it. That
directly attacks the residual no amount of self-review can, at the cost of
reconciling two formalisations' board encodings — days, not weeks, and
cheap relative to what it buys: the first evidence about spec correctness
that does not pass through this study's single author.

**8.5 The Lean-to-Rocq import path is a spike, not a plan.** The tempting
composite — reuse Chess.lean *and* get certified extraction via
**rocq-lean-import** (<https://github.com/rocq-community/rocq-lean-import>)
— is the path our verification of that tool's own documentation says is most
likely to fail: it is an explicitly experimental alpha, its README states
the translation **breaks extraction** for some constructs, the Lean stdlib
passes only with conversion checking disabled, and mathlib is intractable
(11,867 of 66,400 entries skipped even with errors tolerated). The correct
next step is a bounded spike (≤1 session): does Chess.lean's dependency set
survive `lean4export` plus the importer at all, and does Rocq extraction
produce OCaml for the chess definitions specifically? Any red answer falls
back to 8.3's Rocq-native spec with Chess.lean kept in its 8.4 oracle role.
Attractive enough to spike, too fragile to plan around.

**8.6 Cheap contract-side hardening from this study's own failures.** Three
smaller changes each close a defect this study observed. *Minimal-repair
obligation:* make "the smallest change satisfying the violated clause" an
explicit, diff-size-budgeted contract obligation — this targets the bug01
redesign fork directly and costs a prompt line and a gate check; its risk is
that it also forbids the beneficial latent-defect fix Arm A's bug06 run
produced, so it needs the organic-bonus lane to remain a sanctioned exit.
*Specification mutation testing:* bug04 proved a clause can be vacuous
against an entire fault class (global sign inversion satisfies the mirror
identity exactly); systematically mutating the *twin* and requiring each
mutation to trip a clause would have found that vacuity at build time for
roughly the cost of one bridge run per mutation class. *Invocation-capped
review:* §4.3 shows rounds-capping still granted one arm 1.8× the reviewer
invocations; capping invocations, not rounds, makes the review budget an
actual budget. Each of these is testable in a case02 at marginal cost,
because none changes the experimental harness.

**8.7 Instrument the fix/verification split.** This study cannot say what
fraction of FCDD's 3–5× premium buys verification rather than repair,
because cells report only totals. Timestamping the moment the fix first
passes the acceptance script — trivially loggable by the harness — would
split every cell into *time-to-fix* and *verification tail*. That number
decides whether future effort should go to cheaper proving (8.3) or faster
localisation, and it is the number a practitioner budgeting FCDD adoption
actually needs.

## 9. Conclusions

1. Both methods reliably repaired real assembly defects; every cell under
   the final instrument produced an acceptance-passing fix.
2. **Goal 2 (cost) is answered.** FCDD was more expensive on 7 of 7 matched
   pairs (*p* = 0.016, contingent on one disclosed imputation; direction
   robust regardless), median ratio 4.74× on raw tokens and ≈3.3–3.4× on
   priced bases, interval [1.12, 6.88], and its upfront contract never
   amortised.
3. **Goal 1 (predictability) is not answered, and we decline to pretend
   otherwise.** The across-defect dispersion contrast rests on a single
   cell; the replicated cell shows the predicted shape — FCDD single-mode,
   the ordinary arm forking between a minimal fix and a below-gate-floor
   redesign — at a depth where no test can reach significance (Fisher
   *p* = 0.40; attainable floor 0.10) and under an oracle asymmetry that
   predicts the same shape without any method effect. It is a hypothesis
   with a mechanism and an instrument, not a finding.
4. **No quality claim is made.** Under the counterbalanced regrade FCDD was
   favoured on 6 of 7 pairs (*p* = 0.125) and the paired composite CI
   [−0.44, +0.78] spans zero; excluding one pair the arms tie exactly, and
   the grading was label-blind but not treatment-blind. What survives is
   narrow: identical or equivalent fixes, better *evidence* — and one
   demonstration that the sealed gate cannot distinguish a careful repair
   from a risky redesign, which is the strongest argument in this data for
   contract-shaped instruments even if not for the contract's price.
5. **Measurement discipline dominates.** Report reviewer counts, reconcile
   costs against transcripts, control the review termination rule, randomise
   grading order, and re-derive every number before every draft — we failed
   each of these at least once, and each failure produced a
   publishable-looking result that was false.
